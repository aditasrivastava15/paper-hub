# Before the papers

A recursive transformer gets a deeper computation without storing a deeper model.

In a normal transformer, every layer has its own weights. Extra depth means extra stored weights. In a recursive transformer, one block is stored once and applied several times. The numbers moving through that block are the hidden state, the model’s working draft. Each pass updates the draft with the same weights.

Suppose the block is k layers deep and runs L times. The model then performs about k times L layer-steps and stores only about k layers of weights. The amount of work and the size of the model come apart.

The split is between what the model keeps and what the model does. Parameters are the saved settings, the weights stored in memory. Compute is the work of using those settings. That work is counted in FLOPs, short for floating-point operations: each small multiply or add. The numbers being updated are called the residual stream. It is the model’s working draft, one long list of numbers for each word. Chain-of-thought buys more of this work by writing extra words. The model writes one reasoning word, then runs the whole network again to write the next. A loop buys more of the same work without those extra words. The same saved weights, called tied weights because they are shared, edit the draft again. Effective depth is how many times the layers actually run. It grows with each loop. The number of saved weights does not. The abstracts only introduce this second route. The full papers have to show when another edit still improves the draft, and when more edits start to ruin a draft that was already right. Researchers call that last failure overthinking.

These notes follow that idea through three abstracts, in the order it appeared: a shared block, a loop used for reasoning, and the same loop inside a language model. The picture above comes from a primer. The abstracts are where the authors first state it.

## Three abstracts, three chapters

Universal Transformers, by Dehghani and coauthors (2018), starts from a weakness of the ordinary Transformer. Attention can look at a whole sequence at once, which makes training fast. It does not keep revising that sequence. Simple jobs, such as copying a string or following a short logical pattern, break once the input is longer than the training examples. The abstract describes a model that applies the same attention and the same small update again and again, to every position at the same time. Some positions can stop early. Others keep going. The authors report better results than a standard Transformer on algorithmic tasks and on the LAMBADA reading task, plus 0.9 BLEU on WMT14 English-to-German translation. Under the assumptions in the paper, this model can simulate a general computer. A Transformer with a fixed number of layers cannot. The new idea is repetition across depth. It is not the older kind of recurrence that steps from one token to the next.

Reasoning with Latent Thoughts, by Saunshi, Dikkala, Li, Kumar, and Reddi, aims the same idea at reasoning. Latent means the thinking stays inside the number-lists, with no extra words written out. The claim is stronger than “shared weights save memory.” A hard reasoning problem often needs many steps. It does not always need a new set of saved weights for every step. The authors test this on made-up tasks: addition, multi-hop lookup (following a fact through several links, such as “the singer of this song, then that person’s spouse”), and math problems. A block of k layers, run L times, almost matches a taller model that has k times L separate layers, each with its own weights. The looped block clearly beats those same k layers when they run only once. The abstract says a similar pattern shows up in real language models. What lines up with the score is the effective depth, the number of layer-steps that actually run, not the number of unique layers stored. That looks like the improvement people get by writing a longer chain of thought. The authors also say they prove a direct swap: T loops can do the job of T written reasoning steps. A loop here buys reasoning depth without a matching stack of new layers.

Scaling up Test-Time Compute with Latent Reasoning, by Geiping and coauthors, turns that loop into a language model that can be trained. The model repeats one middle block and can repeat it more times after training, at test time. Other reasoning models get extra compute by writing more tokens. This one does not need special chain-of-thought training data. It can work in a short context window. It can also hold reasoning steps that are hard to write in words. The reported model has 3.5 billion parameters and was trained on 800 billion tokens. Reasoning scores improve as the number of loops grows, sometimes by a lot, up to a compute budget the authors compare with a model of about 50 billion parameters. After training, the loop count is a dial: more laps, more hidden thinking, and no long trail of intermediate words.

## What the abstracts already show

The story in the abstracts is short. In 2018, repeating one block fixes simple tasks that break on longer inputs, and it supports a claim about general computation. The latent-thoughts paper uses the same repeat to replace a tall stack of unique layers, and to do work that a written chain of thought would otherwise do. Geiping’s paper trains the repeat on purpose, then runs it longer at test time, on a model big enough that the claim is about language-model compute rather than a toy sequence task.

The three papers are read to check whether that story survives the experiments, the training setup, and the failures. The notes that follow are that reading. This page is only the starting picture, taken from a primer and from three abstracts.
